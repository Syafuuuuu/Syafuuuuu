# Website Direction

## Working Concept

A personal creative archive with the tactile character of an old studio instrument.

The website should feel like opening a beautifully kept notebook, switching on a hi-fi console, or discovering a well-loved camera in a workshop. It is personal, observant, slightly eccentric, and carefully made. It should not feel like a software portfolio, a product landing page, or a polished personal brand template.

The visual direction is **skeuomorphic retro-modern**: physical controls, layered materials, warm light, analogue imperfections, and readable modern geometry brought together in one coherent personal space.

## Core Objective

Create a website that gives visitors a sense of who I am through the things I notice, make, collect, photograph, and think about.

The site should:

- Present me as a creative person, designer, photographer, and artist.
- Make room for personal expression rather than professional positioning.
- Showcase photography and visual work with enough space to be experienced properly.
- Collect short writing, observations, references, and fragments of daily life.
- Communicate taste through curation, composition, materials, and typography.
- Encourage slow exploration instead of pushing visitors through a conversion funnel.
- Feel authored and memorable, even when the content is simple.

## What This Website Is Not

- A data science portfolio.
- A corporate résumé or case-study dashboard.
- A futuristic technology landing page.
- A generic minimalist grid with interchangeable cards.
- A dark neon interface covered in glowing lines.
- A social media feed that rewards constant posting.
- An overly precious art gallery that makes personal writing feel inaccessible.

The old identity may still inform the care given to systems and interaction, but the subject is now the person: creative work, visual culture, personal interests, and lived experience.

## Emotional Qualities

The experience should feel:

- Tactile
- Warm
- Curious
- Nostalgic without being retro cosplay
- Intelligent without being corporate
- Personal without being confessional
- Precise without being sterile
- Playful in small, deliberate moments
- Calm enough to linger in

A useful test: the site should feel like it belongs to someone who has opinions about paper, cameras, chairs, records, light, typography, rooms, and the way objects age.

## Visual Language

### Materials

Use material cues to give the interface a sense of physical presence:

- Brushed metal, painted steel, smoked glass, paper, leather, rubber, and translucent plastic.
- Fine grain and subtle noise rather than perfectly flat surfaces.
- Thin seams, inset panels, bezels, labels, screws, edge highlights, and shadowed gaps.
- Slight variation in texture and tone so the interface feels assembled rather than generated.
- Photographs and writing should remain the primary content; material styling should frame them, not compete with them.

Texture must stay restrained. It should be noticed as atmosphere, not read as a filter placed over the entire page.

### Color Direction

Move away from cyan-and-purple neon. Use a warm, object-focused palette with one or two controlled signal colors.

Suggested foundation:

- Warm ivory or aged paper for primary surfaces.
- Charcoal, espresso, or deep graphite for dark panels and type.
- Muted olive, tobacco, rust, ochre, or faded blue as supporting colors.
- Soft amber, cream, or red-orange for active indicators and highlights.
- Occasional CRT green or muted orange as a small retro reference, never as the dominant palette.

The palette should resemble a studio, darkroom, listening room, or instrument panel rather than a gaming interface.

### Typography

Typography should combine editorial personality with industrial clarity.

- Use an expressive serif or humanist display face for titles, notes, and personal statements.
- Pair it with a practical grotesk, humanist sans, or restrained mono for labels, metadata, navigation, and controls.
- Use uppercase labels sparingly, as if they belong to a physical panel.
- Let typography establish hierarchy through scale, rhythm, and contrast rather than excessive decoration.
- Avoid default system stacks and avoid making every heading look like a futuristic machine readout.

The title type should feel like a publication or record sleeve. The utility type should feel like a camera marking, archive label, or studio control.

## Interaction Principles

The interface should make interaction feel physical without becoming difficult to use.

### Controls

Use familiar controls with tactile styling:

- Toggle switches for modes or display preferences.
- Raised buttons with a clear pressed state.
- Rotary-dial-inspired controls only where they communicate a real choice.
- Tabs, sliders, and segmented controls for browsing content.
- Small indicator lights for status, selected sections, or ambient feedback.
- Tooltips or labels for unusual controls.

Every physical metaphor must still behave like a normal accessible web control. Do not hide navigation behind decorative mechanisms.

### Feedback

Interactions should have satisfying but quiet feedback:

- A button should depress slightly.
- A toggle should move with a short mechanical transition.
- A panel may brighten or shift when selected.
- Page transitions can use a brief scan, fade, or mechanical slide.
- Images can reveal captions or metadata through a controlled overlay.

Avoid constant motion. Animation should communicate state, provide orientation, or make the interface feel alive.

### Browsing

Support both intentional navigation and happy accidents.

Visitors should be able to:

- Browse a curated photography collection.
- Open a single image and read its context.
- Move through notes or fragments chronologically or by theme.
- Discover related entries through tags, references, or shared subjects.
- Return easily to the archive without losing their place.

## Content Direction

### Photography

Photography should be a central pillar of the site, not a decorative gallery section.

Each image may include:

- Title or short caption.
- Date and place when meaningful.
- Camera, film, or technical details when they add interest.
- A sentence about what drew attention to the scene.
- Optional related images or a small sequence.

Do not force every image into the same aspect ratio. A curated mix of landscape, portrait, contact-sheet, and detail views can make the archive feel more like a real body of work.

### Notes and Fragments

Include short, low-pressure writing such as:

- Things noticed during a walk.
- A paragraph about an object, room, song, film, book, or image.
- Brief reflections on making and looking.
- Small records of places visited.
- Process notes and unfinished thoughts.
- Lists of things currently being enjoyed or studied.

These entries do not need to resolve into lessons. The value is in the voice, specificity, and point of view.

### Extracts of Things I Like

Create a space for references and influences:

- Books, films, music, artists, designers, photographers, objects, and places.
- Short explanations of why something matters.
- Visual pairings, quotations, scans, or collected details where appropriate.
- A sense of an evolving personal index rather than a definitive ranking system.

### About

The About section should be direct and human. It should explain how I see and make things without falling back into job-title language.

It can include:

- A short introduction.
- Current interests.
- Creative practices.
- Places, tools, and references that shape the work.
- A portrait or self-image that feels natural to the visual world of the site.

## Suggested Information Architecture

### Home / Field Notes

The landing page should immediately establish the atmosphere and voice. It can combine a featured image, a short statement, a recent note, and small signals pointing into the archive.

### Archive

A browsable collection of photography, notes, references, and visual fragments. Use filters or sections only when they genuinely help discovery.

### Photography

A focused image experience with individual entries and optional metadata.

### Notes

Short writing, observations, lists, and personal documentation.

### Things I Like

A curated index of influences, objects, media, and references.

### About

A personal introduction and a simple way to get in touch.

The final navigation can remain smaller than this list. These are content areas, not a requirement for a large multi-page application.

## Layout Direction

Use a composition that feels editorial and assembled:

- Strong margins and intentional whitespace around images and writing.
- Asymmetrical arrangements balanced by a clear underlying grid.
- Occasional full-bleed image moments alongside compact labelled panels.
- Side notes, captions, stamps, and metadata used as supporting details.
- Layered surfaces that create depth without turning every section into a card.
- Clear transitions between archive areas, like turning pages or moving between instrument panels.

Avoid a page made entirely from floating rounded cards. Use framed modules only when they represent a real object, archive entry, control panel, or grouped piece of content.

## Sound and Motion

Sound should not autoplay. If sound is ever introduced, it should be optional and clearly controlled.

Motion should include:

- A restrained page-load reveal.
- Tactile press and toggle responses.
- Gentle image or panel transitions.
- Optional CRT-like scan or flicker details used sparingly.
- Respect for `prefers-reduced-motion`.

The page should remain comfortable on mobile devices and should not depend on hover to reveal essential content.

## Accessibility and Usability

The nostalgic visual language must not reduce usability.

- Maintain readable contrast.
- Use semantic HTML and real interactive elements.
- Provide alt text for meaningful images.
- Keep navigation visible and understandable.
- Ensure controls work with keyboard and touch.
- Do not rely on color alone to communicate state.
- Keep text comfortable to read across screen sizes.
- Preserve the content hierarchy when textures, shadows, or decorative elements are removed.

## Technical Direction

The current technical foundation can remain lightweight:

- FastAPI for the backend.
- Jinja2 for server-rendered templates.
- Plain CSS for the visual system.
- Vanilla JavaScript for interaction and animation.
- Static image assets organized as a small personal archive.

The redesign should prioritize a strong content model and visual system before introducing additional frameworks. New libraries should only be added when they provide a clear benefit to the browsing experience, media handling, or accessibility.

## Definition of Success

The redesign is successful when:

1. A visitor understands within seconds that this is a personal creative world, not a professional technology portfolio.
2. The visual language feels tactile and distinctive without becoming a costume.
3. Photography, writing, and references feel equally intentional.
4. The interface rewards exploration but remains easy to navigate.
5. The site feels recognizably personal even before the visitor reads the About section.
6. New entries can be added without redesigning the whole page.
7. The design still works when the decorative texture, animation, and retro details are removed.

## Short Creative Brief

Build a personal website that feels like a warm, tactile creative instrument: part studio notebook, part photo archive, part listening-room console. Replace the old futuristic data-science identity with a personal world of images, observations, references, and fragments. Use skeuomorphic retro-modern details such as raised controls, inset panels, analogue labels, material textures, and soft mechanical feedback, but keep the content human, editorial, accessible, and easy to explore.
